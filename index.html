Worldwide

I finished second in the world designing the piece of DNA that tells a drug where to turn on.

Synthetic enhancers are short DNA sequences that decide where a gene therapy fires. Get one right and the therapy hits hard inside the target cell and stays silent everywhere else. Gosai et al. showed in 2024 that a deep learning model, Malinois, could design such enhancers from scratch, predicting a 200-base sequence’s activity in a given cell line; Exonic pushed the same model past the range its training data covered and built the strongest synthetic enhancer found so far. The tournament opens that problem up: write 200 bases, let the models score them on target and off, and try to beat everyone else’s.

Drug discovery usually means screening: make a million candidates, test them all, keep what works. Screening does not dent a space of 4200 sequences, which is more than there are atoms in the observable universe. But a model of a cell is differentiable. You can ask what happens to the gene if base 72 becomes a G and get an exact number back instead of an experiment, so I did not screen for a sequence, I wrote one: take the gradient, step, repeat, the way directed evolution works in a lab but at a generation per microsecond. The catch is that a single model will cheerfully call gibberish the strongest enhancer ever built, the same blind spot that makes a sticker turn a stop sign into a speed limit, so every candidate had to survive a committee of models rather than please one.
